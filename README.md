# LPS Master Prepper List

This is primarily a product guide for disaster preparedness supplies, organized by where you'd keep them: the go bag first, then the home, then the car, followed by situational gear and bigger purchases. We'll then cover a few non-product topics (get to know your neighbors!) in broad strokes. Food and clothing get only short rules of thumb, without product picks.

Each item in this guide has one to three product suggestions:

- Top pick: The top of the line, though it may be pricey.
- Mid pick: A balance of quality and cost-effectiveness.
- Low pick: The ultra-budget option that still (probably) works well enough.

Not every product uses all three tiers. For some items, the top pick is already cheap enough, or there aren't any good substitutes that you'd want to rely on in an emergency. Generic items that you can buy at a grocery store or pharmacy (e.g. soap, garbage bags) aren't given specific picks and don't use the tier system.

Most product links go to Amazon or Walmart. Prices change often; a \~ marks an estimate that wasn't checked against a current listing.

Note: Most of this document was written by Claude, and many of the products are Claude's recommendations. Claude was asked to favor established, time-tested products, and based its picks on retailer listings and published reviews. The LPS team has reviewed Claude's picks, though may not be able to personally vouch for them.

## Tags

Products can have any combination of the "LPS" tags: Lindy, Pareto, and Schelling, which represent three ideals when selecting a product. Don't take these tags too seriously; they're mostly just for fun.

- **\[Lindy\]** is about time: the product has been around for decades, and things that have lasted a long time tend to keep lasting. It says nothing about price or whether the design is still the best. Further reading: [Lindy effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindy_effect)
- **\[Pareto\]** is about value per dollar: most of what the best option does, for much less money. It's the knee of the price curve, where spending more stops buying much. Further reading: [Pareto principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pareto_principle)
- **\[Schelling\]** is about salience: it's the default people land on without coordinating, the one everyone recommends. Its value comes partly from that: easy to find, lots of accessories and advice, and spares or parts other people can share. Further reading: [Schelling point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focal_point_(game_theory))

The eight possible combinations of these three tags, each with an example:

- **No tags: Morakniv Garberg.** The top fixed-blade pick and an excellent knife, but it only came out in 2016, the Mora most people name is the cheap Companion, and it costs about six times as much. Plenty of good picks carry no tags; they're just good at their job. The bad version: a "200-piece survival kit" from an unknown brand, which is new, nobody's default, and poor value once you notice most of the pieces are filler.
- **\[Lindy\] only: Fisher Space Pen.** Sold since the 1960s and still excellent, but it's a niche pick and costs far more than a pencil that also writes on wet paper. The bad version: the Maglite, in police cars and kitchen drawers since 1979 with the same basic design. It's heavy, runs on D cells, and is far dimmer than a modern LED light half its size, so it's neither the current default nor good value.
- **\[Pareto\] only: Wurkkos FC11C.** Brighter, better-colored light than lights costing three times as much, for about $20. But the brand is only a few years old, and most people outside flashlight forums haven't heard of it. The bad version: a cheap open-frame generator from a little-known brand, which gives about twice the watts per dollar of an inverter generator. But it's loud enough to hear down the block, burns through fuel quickly, and its rougher power can damage sensitive electronics.
- **\[Schelling\] only: Energizer Ultimate Lithium.** The default battery for gear that sits in storage for years, but it costs several times as much as alkalines. The bad version: the LifeStraw, the water filter everyone has heard of. You can only drink through it, not fill a bottle or cook with it, and it isn't old enough to be Lindy.
- **\[Lindy\] + \[Pareto\]: JanSport backpack.** Made the same simple way since the 1970s, cheap, with a lifetime warranty. But it isn't what people name for a go bag; that's usually a pack with a hip belt.
- **\[Lindy\] + \[Schelling\]: Honda EU2200i.** Honda's quiet inverter generators have been the reference for decades and are what most people recommend. But a Champion does most of the job for about half the price, so it isn't the value pick.
- **\[Pareto\] + \[Schelling\]: Sawyer Squeeze.** The default backpacking filter and cheap for what it does. It has only been around for a bit over a decade, so it isn't Lindy yet.
- **All three: BIC lighter.** Sold since 1973, the lighter everyone grabs by default, and about the most reliable fire you can buy for a dollar or two.

One more tag works differently: **\[Endorsed\]** marks a product the LPS team has bought and used. 

## Supply watch

Last updated Nov 21, 2026. A wave of emergency buying followed the mid-November tech outages, and some items on this list are hard to find. Each line names where the item sits in the list and what to buy if it's gone. Shortages after a scare usually ease within weeks to a few months, so wait out clearly inflated prices where you can, except for things you need before winter, like heat.

### Scarce now

- **Water storage containers** (Reliance Aqua-Tainer; Home essentials → Water). If sold out: Scepter 5-gallon water cans, WaterBricks, food-grade 5-gallon buckets with lids, or refilled 2-liter soda bottles. Any food-grade container works; the brand doesn't matter.
- **Hand-crank NOAA weather radios** (Go bag → Communication). If sold out: any AM/FM radio that runs on AA batteries, plus the walkie-talkies' NOAA weather channels, Wireless Emergency Alerts on your phone, and the car radio.
- **Solar power stations** (Big purchases). If sold out: a smaller station from any established brand, power banks for phones and lights, or the car kit's inverter with the engine running outdoors.
- **Generators** (Big purchases). If sold out: a power station, or wait for restocks rather than paying inflated prices. Whatever you get, run it outdoors only, at least 20 ft from windows and doors.
- **Water purification tablets** (Go bag → Water). If sold out: plain unscented bleach (8 drops per gallon of clear water, then wait 30 minutes), or boil water for 1 minute.
- **Emergency food such as MREs** (Home essentials → Food). If sold out: ordinary canned and dry groceries you already eat.

### Likely to sell out (Claude's analysis)

- **Water filters** (Sawyer Squeeze, Sawyer gravity system; Go bag → Water, Home essentials → Water). Why: water is the first thing new preppers buy, most guides recommend the same few Sawyer models, and purification tablets, the companion purchase, are already selling out. Fallbacks: other hollow-fiber squeeze filters such as the Katadyn BeFree or Platypus QuickDraw, or bleach and boiling.
- **Bathtub water liner** (waterBOB; Situational). Why: it's a single product from one maker, cheap enough to buy on impulse, and recommended in nearly every outage and hurricane guide. Fallback: scrub the tub and fill it anyway for washing and flushing, and fill every clean container you have for drinking.
- **Indoor-safe propane heaters and 1-lb propane cylinders** (Mr. Heater Buddy; Home essentials → Warmth). Why: it's winter, Walmart already reports higher propane sales, and the Buddy line is the one common indoor-safe propane heater, so demand piles onto a single product. The cylinders also fuel camp stoves. Fallbacks: a hose and adapter to run the heater and the camp stove from a 20-lb tank; for warmth, one closed-off room with window film, hot-water bottles and sleeping bags. Never use a camp stove or outdoor heater indoors.
- **Potassium iodide** (Situational). Why: only a few FDA-approved brands exist, made in small batches, and it sold out within days during past nuclear scares (Fukushima in 2011, the start of the Ukraine war in 2022). No real substitute, and skip non-FDA "iodine" supplements. Check whether your state hands it out free near nuclear plants; sheltering matters more anyway.
- **N95 and P100 masks** (Go bag → Protective gear). Why: masks sold out in 2020 and during the 2023 wildfire smoke, and any pandemic news would bring that back quickly. Fallback: any NIOSH-approved N95 from another maker (Honeywell, Moldex and others); check approval on the CDC's NIOSH list, and avoid unapproved "KN95" masks, which are often counterfeit.
- **Trauma supplies** (tourniquet, chest seals, hemostatic gauze; Go bag → First aid kit). Why: genuine products come from a few makers through authorized distributors, which can't ramp up quickly when demand jumps. No substitute. Fakes flood in during shortages, so buy only from the maker or an authorized distributor, even if that means waiting.
- **Satellite messengers** (Garmin inReach; Big purchases). Why: they're expensive, so stores keep few in stock, and a wave of buying set off by telecom and internet failures pushes people toward ways to call for help without a cell network. Fallbacks: a personal locator beacon, or a phone with built-in satellite SOS.
- **Bucket toilet seat** (Reliance Luggable Loo; Home essentials → Hygiene & sanitation). Why: it's a specific product that's bulky enough that stores stock only a few, and it's the standard pick for sheltering at home without running water. Fallback: any 5-gallon bucket with a tight lid, lined with contractor bags and kitty litter.

## Go bag

Everything you carry if you have to leave. Pack compact or travel-size versions where they exist (a small soap, a wrap of duct tape around a water bottle).

Plan on one bag per person. Each adult carries their own, and kids old enough to walk can carry a small one with a comfort item, a snack, a headlamp, and a card with their parents' names and phone numbers in case you're separated.

  - Top pick: [Platypus SoftBottle](https://www.amazon.com/Platypus-SoftBottles-with-Closure-Cap/dp/B00ZX0ERE2) (1L or 2L). A 20-year-old design that rolls up flat when empty; its standard threads fit the Sawyer Squeeze. **$15** \[Lindy\]

### Light

Standardize on AA and AAA so one battery stock covers the whole bag. A light that takes either a 14500 (a lithium-ion rechargeable cell the size of an AA) or a plain AA gets the best of both: bright on the rechargeable, and still running on the household AA stock. Only put a 14500 in a light that says it supports one: it runs at 3.7 volts versus 1.5 for an AA, so it can destroy an AA-only light. Never put 14500s in the two-AA lights below or in the Siege lantern. The Eneloop charger on this list doesn't charge them, so buy 14500s with built-in USB-C charging or a charger that handles lithium-ion too.

The flashlight and headlamp entries each come in two groups. The AA and AAA lights run on the shared battery stock and keep working as long as you have cells. The lithium-ion lights are far brighter but need recharging from the power bank, solar or a power station, and use cells (18650 or 21700) that nothing else in the bag takes. Their headline lumen figures are turbo bursts: a 4,000-lumen light holds that for a minute or less before heat drops it to roughly 1,000–2,000, still far brighter than any AA light.

Note that the I, author of this guide, am a huge flashlight enthusiast. If you know me IRL, I'd be happy to personally suggest a flashlight to you that suits your personality. Also note that it pains me to suggest any of the mass-market Energizer products, but I admit they're cheap and it's hard to make a truly bad flashlight nowadays.

The standard version has a high-CRI Nichia LED at up to 550 lumens; a 1,000-lumen version is also sold. - **Flashlight (lithium-ion)**

Pocket-size on a single 18650, at a fraction of the TS27's size and price; pack a charged spare cell. **$20-35** \[Pareto\] \[Endorsed\]

### Clothing

Rules of thumb rather than product picks, since clothes depend on size, climate and taste.

- **One full change of clothes,** packed in a dry bag or zip-top bag. Choose wool or synthetics, not cotton: cotton stays wet and pulls heat out of you, while wool and synthetics keep you warm even when damp. Long sleeves and long pants double as cleanup clothes.
- **Extra socks: 2–3 pairs of wool.** Wet feet cause blisters and misery faster than almost anything else. Darn Tough wool socks come with a lifetime guarantee and are the default pick among hikers (\~$25 a pair).
- **A warm layer** (fleece or puffy jacket), plus a hat and gloves in cold seasons.
- **Sturdy, broken-in shoes.** You may have to walk a long way, and new boots give you blisters.
- **Swap the bag's clothes twice a year** for the season, and check kids' sizes, since they outgrow them fast.

### Food

A rule of thumb rather than product picks.

- **How much:** about 2,000 calories per adult per day; more for active adults or anyone clearing debris (up to about 3,000), less for small children.
- **How long:** the Red Cross guideline is 3 days of food for evacuation and 2 weeks at home.
- **What to store:** shelf-stable food you already eat, rotated into normal meals so nothing expires: "store what you eat, eat what you store."
- **Mind the water:** food that needs cooking water eats into your water supply. Rice, pasta and freeze-dried meals all need it (a freeze-dried meal takes about 2 cups), while canned food brings its own water. Salty food makes you thirstier. If water is tight, lean toward canned and no-cook food.
- **Pets:** count their food and water too.
- **In an outage, keep the fridge and freezer closed.** An unopened fridge keeps food safe for about 4 hours, a full freezer for about 48 (24 if half full), per the USDA. Fill empty freezer space with jugs of water: they help it stay cold and double as drinking water. Eat from the fridge first, then the freezer, then the pantry.

## Other topics (beyond stuff to buy)

Many sections here deserve their own comprehensive document. These will at least make a good starting point. Some may not be applicable to you depending on your household, skills, and where you live.

### Community

Neighbors are usually the first help after a disaster. Nothing here costs much, but it multiplies everything else on the list. Research backs this up: Daniel Aldrich's *Building Resilience* found that ties between neighbors predicted how well communities recovered better than money or aid did, and Eric Klinenberg's *Heat Wave* found that isolated people died at far higher rates in Chicago's 1995 heat wave.

- Know your neighbors, and swap phone numbers
- Learn who nearby has a generator, medical training, a chainsaw or a truck
- Check on older or isolated neighbors
- Join or start a neighborhood group or CERT team
- Agree on a meeting spot for your street
- Keep a small sharing stock, separate from your own supply: extra water bottles, rice and beans, guest toiletry kits and a spare flashlight or two. Then you can help neighbors without cutting into your household's supply or showing anyone your full stockpile. Share early in a crisis and through a neighborhood group, before things get tense, rather than becoming known as the house with everything.

### Financial resilience

Recovery often costs more than the supplies. Money and paperwork decide how fast you can rebuild.

- An emergency fund (a common target is three to six months of expenses)
- Cash in small bills, since card readers and ATMs need power
- A second bank account at an unrelated bank, so one bank's outage or a frozen account doesn't cut you off from your money
- A credit card on a different network from your main one (Mastercard if your main card is Visa)
- A review of your insurance: standard homeowner's policies usually exclude flood and earthquake
- Renter's insurance, if you rent
- Copies of policies and account numbers in the document bag

### Medical beyond first aid

The first aid kits cover injuries. This covers keeping someone stable through an illness or a chronic condition when the pharmacy or doctor is out of reach.

- A longer prescription supply, where your doctor and insurance allow (many plans permit a 90-day fill)
- A cooler plan for medicines that need refrigeration
- A blood pressure cuff (the pulse oximeter is in the pandemic sick-room kit under Situational)
- Spare antibiotics, prescribed by a doctor for your household (services such as Jase Medical do this), with written instructions for when and how to take each one; don't take them without that guidance
- A temporary dental filling kit and dental floss
- Spare reading glasses, plus a copy of your glasses prescription
- Hearing aid, insulin pump or other medical device batteries and supplies
- A written list of each person's conditions, medications and doses

### Infants, older relatives and pets

Anyone in the household with special needs needs their own kit. These supplies are the hardest to find during a disaster.

- Infants: formula (ready-to-feed needs no clean water), bottles, diapers, wipes, a carrier
- Older relatives: mobility aids, spare canes or walkers, incontinence supplies, any comfort items
- Medical equipment that runs on power, and a plan to keep it running: which power station or generator runs which device, and for how long. Many utilities let you register a household member who depends on powered medical equipment, which can bring outage warnings or priority help; ask yours.
- Pets: food, medications, a leash or carrier for each, records and a recent photo
- A list of pet-friendly shelters or hotels along your evacuation route

### Skills & training

Gear is only as useful as the person using it. Many of these classes are free or cheap through the Red Cross, fire departments and community colleges.

- First aid and CPR
- Stop the Bleed (teaches the tourniquet and wound packing in the trauma kit)
- CERT (Community Emergency Response Team) training
- Practice with the gear you own: set up the tent, run the generator, test the radios
- Basic home repairs: shutting off utilities, patching a roof or window
- Navigation with a map and compass

### Longer-range radio

The FRS walkie-talkies in the bag reach a mile or two. A GMRS license (one FCC fee covers your immediate family, no exam) allows stronger radios and repeaters; an amateur (ham) radio license takes an exam but can reach across a region or farther.

- A GMRS license and GMRS radios, handheld or mobile
- An amateur radio license (the entry-level exam is multiple choice)
- Meshtastic radios: small, cheap LoRa radios that pair with a phone over Bluetooth to send text messages with no cell service and no license. Range is a few miles between radios, farther where there's a local mesh network; buy at least two (\~$30–60 each)
- A local repeater list and a written family channel plan
- External antennas and spare batteries
- A local radio club or community emergency group to practice with

### Hunting & fishing

Useful mainly if you already hunt or fish, or plan to learn; gear without the skill and a license does little good. Check your state's rules: most require a license for each, and hunting often requires a safety course first. The fishing kit is under Situational.

- Hunting and fishing licenses, renewed each year
- A hunter safety course (often required for a first license)
- A rifle, shotgun or bow suited to local game, and a safe or locked storage for it
- Ammunition or arrows, and practice time
- Field-dressing knife, gloves and game bags
- Spare line, hooks, sinkers and lures
- A cooler or ice plan for keeping meat and fish

### Growing & preserving food

This is about producing and storing food rather than stockpiling it (the stockpiling rule of thumb is under Home essentials → Food). A garden takes a season or two to learn, and canning has real food-safety rules, so start before you need either.

- Seeds that store well and grow in your climate (open-pollinated varieties can be saved year to year)
- Basic garden tools: spade, hoe, rake, hose or watering can
- Raised beds, containers or a cold frame if space or soil is limited
- A pressure canner (required for low-acid foods like vegetables and meat) and a water-bath canner
- Jars, lids and rings; lids are single-use, so stock extras
- A food dehydrator
- A tested canning guide from a university extension or the USDA

### Local hazards

The biggest risks depend on where you live. Check your county or state emergency management site for the hazards that apply to you.

- Earthquake: straps for the water heater and tall furniture, latches for cabinets, a wrench by the gas meter
- Flood: a battery backup for the sump pump, check valves, raised storage for valuables
- Wildfire: a cleared defensible space around the house, ember-resistant vents, a go-bag by the door
- Hurricane: window protection, a generator plan, and a known evacuation zone
- Extreme heat: a cool-room plan, fans, shade, and a list of local cooling centers
- Winter storms: pipe insulation, a plan for heat without power, roof snow removal. If the house will drop below freezing in an outage, let faucets drip; for a long outage, shut off the main water valve and drain the pipes so they don't burst.
- Apartments and high-rises: when the power goes out, elevators stop, and so do the pumps that push water to upper floors. Store more water, and plan for getting people, pets and supplies up and down the stairs.

### Local compute (offline AI)

An AI model running on your own computer keeps working without internet. It can answer reference questions, translate, summarize manuals, draft documents and help with repairs or code. Treat its answers as a starting point, not an authority: local models can be confidently wrong, so check anything medical, electrical or safety-related against a real reference.

- A computer with plenty of memory: model size is limited mostly by RAM or GPU memory, and 32 GB or more opens up much more capable models
- Open-weight models downloaded ahead of time, in a few sizes, including a small one that runs fast on a laptop
- The software to run them, plus offline installers, saved before you need it
- An external drive with copies of the models and installers
- A power plan: a laptop sips power, while a desktop GPU can draw several hundred watts, which matters on a power station or solar
- Offline speech-to-text and translation models
- Offline Wikipedia and the reference library above, which pair well with a local model
- A test run with the network unplugged, so you know it works offline

## Plans & upkeep

Nothing to buy here, but the gear depends on it.

- Take a first aid and Stop the Bleed class (the tourniquet and hemostatic gauze need practice)
- Find and label the gas, water and electric shutoffs
- Sign up for local emergency alerts
- Prepare your phone: set up Medical ID (iPhone) or Emergency information (Android) so conditions, allergies and contacts show on the lock screen; learn its low-power mode; and write down the AM and FM frequencies of your local emergency broadcast stations
- List your trusted information sources in advance: the local emergency radio station, NOAA, and your county's emergency management office. In a confusing crisis, rumors and fakes spread fast, so be skeptical of anything you can't trace back to an official source.
- Set a reminder every six months to rotate water, medications and batteries, and to swap the go bag's clothes for the season
- Keep sturdy shoes and a flashlight by each bed; after an earthquake or storm, the floor may be covered in broken glass
- Pick a family code word, so you can tell a real call for help from an AI voice clone; share it in person rather than in writing
- Write a one-page trigger sheet: what would make you stay home and what would make you leave, where you'd go (a friend's or relative's place outside the area), two driving routes and a bike route there
- Walk your evacuation route once carrying the loaded go bag, to learn how long it takes and whether the bag is too heavy
- Know that the toilet tank (not the bowl) holds a few gallons of clean backup water, unless you use tank-cleaning tablets

### Regrouping without phones

The hard case is an outage that starts while the household is split up: one person at work, the kids at school, and no phone service. Agree on all of this in advance, write it on the trigger sheet, and practice it once.

- **Three meeting places, in order:** just outside the house (for a fire); a nearby spot if you can't get home, such as a school, library or friend's house; and a place outside the area if the neighborhood itself is unsafe.
- **Default actions,** so nobody has to guess: for example, "if phones are down, go home if it's safe; if you can't, go to meeting place 2."
- **Fixed check-in times.** If someone isn't at the meeting place, come back at set times (say noon and 6 p.m. each day) rather than waiting there indefinitely.
- **Leave a note** in an agreed spot, such as inside the front door: who was there, when, and where they went. The Sharpie and waterproof notebook in the go bag work for this.
- **Know the school's or daycare's reunification plan,** and keep its list of who may pick up your kids current. Schools usually hold children and release them only to listed adults.
- **Decide who picks up whom:** kids, older relatives and pets.
- **An out-of-area contact who relays messages.** When local networks are jammed, long-distance calls and texts often still get through, texts more often than calls. Everyone checks in with that one person; the number goes on the wallet card.
- **A walkie-talkie plan:** a set FRS channel and privacy code for the household.
- **Walk it once:** how long would each person take to walk home from work or school?

**Family in other states.** When relatives live far away, the question is usually whether to travel to them, and where to meet if you can't reach each other. Settle it in advance:

- **Default to staying put.** Long drives in a crisis mean closed roads, fuel shortages and nowhere to stay. Agree on what would justify traveling (your home is unsafe, a family member needs care, a set number of days with no contact) and what wouldn't.
- **Pick one fixed destination,** usually one relative's home in a lower-risk area with water, space and supplies, rather than a midpoint. Without phones, a midpoint is just two people guessing.
- **Travelers come to the destination; the people there stay put,** so nobody crosses paths on the road.
- **Set a waiting period** before anyone sets out (for example, 72 hours with no contact), so a short outage doesn't send everyone onto the highway.
- **Plan the trip:** two routes, the paper road atlas in the car kit, where you can get fuel, how far one tank takes you, cash, and a fallback place to stop overnight. Leave a note at home saying where you went.
- **List backup ways to reach each other,** in order: texts, calls, email, the out-of-area contact, the satellite messenger, and GMRS or ham radio if anyone is licensed.
- **Agree on "don't come" too.** If everyone at the destination is fine, they should be able to tell travelers to stay home.
